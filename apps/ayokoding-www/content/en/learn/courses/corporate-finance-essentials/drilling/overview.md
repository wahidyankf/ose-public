## Recall Q&A

### Q1 (co-01): What is present value, and why is it always below the future sum?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Present value is a future cash flow divided by one plus the discount rate, raised to the wait. It sits below the future sum because money today can earn while it waits and because waiting carries risk, so a later unit must be cheaper now. Example 1 prices 10,000 in five years at 6,805.83 for exactly this reason: the 1.4693280768 factor is the compounded price of waiting. Any valuation that skips discounting overstates worth by the time value it ignores, which is why every project screen in Theme E starts here rather than with raw cash totals.

</details>

### Q2 (co-01): How does a longer wait change present value when the rate stays fixed?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

A longer wait raises the discount factor exponentially, so present value falls faster with each added year. At 8 percent, 10,000 due in five years is worth 6,805.83, but due in ten it would be worth far less because the factor compounds. Time punishes distant promises geometrically, never linearly. That is why distant forecast years barely move a DCF even when their cash looks large. The practical habit is to test any valuation at a longer horizon: if extending the forecast barely moves the answer, the terminal assumption owns the valuation, not the yearly detail.

</details>

### Q3 (co-02): What is future value, and what does compounding add each year?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Future value is present cash multiplied by one plus the earned rate, raised to the horizon. Compounding adds interest on past interest, so growth accelerates: 5,000 at 6 percent reaches 8,954.24 in ten years, more than simple interest would allow. The excess over simple interest is interest earning interest. Savers love it, borrowers pay it, and both should compute it exactly. The Example 2 factor keeps all twenty computed digits because rounding mid-course would leak error into every later year; money prints with two decimals only at the end.

</details>

### Q4 (co-03): What is an annuity, and when does its shortcut formula apply?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

An annuity is a stream of equal payments at regular intervals, valued as one sum through the annuity factor. The shortcut applies only when payments are equal, timing is regular, and the rate is constant. Example 3 values five 2,000 receipts at 7,985.42. Break any of the three conditions and you must discount payment by payment, as Example 22 does. Loans, leases, and level-premium insurance are the three shapes to recognize on sight; everything with uneven payments goes back to payment-by-payment discounting.

</details>

### Q5 (co-04): What does the discount rate represent, and why does risk raise it?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

The discount rate is the price of time plus the price of risk in one number. Risk raises it because uncertain cash must clear a higher bar to be worth holding today. Example 5 shows the same 50,000 worth 35,534.07 at 5 percent but only 18,796.85 at 15 percent. Arguments about value are usually arguments about this rate in disguise. Theme F replaces guessing with construction: a risk-free yield plus beta-scaled market risk for equity, and a tax-shielded coupon for debt, blended by market weights into one defensible hurdle.

</details>

### Q6 (co-04): Why do distant cash flows move most when the rate changes?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

The discount factor compounds with the wait, so a rate change multiplies hardest where the exponent is largest. Example 44 shows each two-point rise erasing roughly fifteen thousand of the van's value, mostly from later years. Long projects fear rate rises precisely because their value lives far out. That is also why growth stocks swing hardest when central banks move: their cash is the most distant, so the same rate change discounts them the most. Duration is sensitivity, and distance is duration.

</details>

### Q7 (co-05): Which income-statement line does each profitability ratio start from?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Gross margin starts from gross profit, net margin from net income, ROA from net income over assets, and ROE from net income over equity. Each rung answers a different question: trading skill, final keep-rate, whole-firm efficiency, or the owners' deal. Misreading the rung corrupts every ratio built on it. Always name the numerator before quoting the ratio, and check whether interest and tax have been removed yet: an EBIT-based figure compared against a net-based peer is a category error, not an insight.

</details>

### Q8 (co-05): Why can gross margin stay flat while net margin falls?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Because costs below the gross line, operations, interest, or tax, can rise while trading holds steady. Merpati keeps 40.00 percent gross but only 9.75 percent net, so overhead is the story. When net trails peers with similar gross margins, investigate operating costs first. The gap between the two margins assigns the blame. Track the pair across quarters rather than once: a narrowing gap means discipline is improving below the gross line, while a widening one means overhead is eating trading gains.

</details>

### Q9 (co-06): What does the current ratio guarantee, and what does it not?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

It guarantees that near-term resources arithmetically cover near-term bills: 2.25 times at Merpati. It does not guarantee the resources convert in time, because inventory may sit unsold. Composition matters as much as total, which is why the quick ratio exists. A ratio sliding toward one deserves action before it arrives there, because restoring liquidity under pressure costs far more than maintaining it early. A comfortable headline with slow shelves is comfort on paper only.

</details>

### Q10 (co-06): Why does the quick ratio exclude inventory?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Inventory is the slowest current asset to convert: it must sell, then be collected, before it pays a bill. Dropping it leaves cash plus receivables, the resources available without a fire sale. Loan covenants often cite the quick ratio instead of the current ratio for exactly this reason: lenders underwrite distress, not averages. Merpati's 1.20 quick ratio against a 2.25 current ratio shows shelves carry the cover. Lenders cite the strict measure because distress never waits for slow stock.

</details>

### Q11 (co-07): When does debt-to-equity mislead, and what fixes it?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

It misleads when readers assume all liabilities are interest-bearing debt, or when they compare firms with different payable practices. Stating which debt counts fixes it: Merpati's 0.76 uses interest-bearing loans only. Pair it with interest coverage, because the stock of debt matters less than the flow that services it. One ratio never prices funding risk alone. Read the stock of debt beside the flow that services it: coverage tells you how far profit can fall before interest binds, which the mix ratio alone cannot say.

</details>

### Q12 (co-07): Why do debt and equity ratios always sum to one?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Both divide the same asset total by funding source, and the two sources exhaust all funding by the balance-sheet identity. Merpati shows 0.50 and 0.50. The sum is a built-in arithmetic receipt: anything else means the snapshot is broken. Assert identities in code before computing ratios on top of them. A balance sheet that does not balance poisons all three ratio families at once, so the check belongs in the program rather than in the analyst's eyeballing.

</details>

### Q13 (co-08): What does asset turnover separate from profitability?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

It separates how hard assets work from how profitable sales are. Merpati turns each asset unit into 1.20 sales units regardless of margin. A busy firm can still be poor and a quiet one rich. Margin times turnover explains return on assets, so a falling ROA is diagnosed instantly: prices slipped, or assets idled. Within one firm, a falling turnover with steady sales means assets are accumulating idly, which is the early warning for over-investment.

</details>

### Q14 (co-08): What action does a receivables-days number suggest?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

It suggests how far collection lags terms, and therefore where to act. Merpati waits 45.62 days against thirty-day terms: tighten credit, chase overdue accounts, or discount early payment. Days are actionable where balances are not. Convert every worrying balance into days before deciding what to fix. The same conversion prices inventory days and payables days, so the pattern matters more than any single instance: divide the stock by its daily flow and read the wait.

</details>

### Q15 (co-09): What is working capital, and what funds it?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Working capital is current assets minus current liabilities: the 250,000 buffer funding day-to-day operations. It is funded by long-term capital, lenders and owners, to the extent it is positive. A negative buffer means short-term bills fund long-term needs, which is fragile. Growth consumes it, which is why fast-growing firms feel cash-poor while profitable. The cash cycle in Example 21 counts exactly how many days that consumption stays funded before collections return it.

</details>

### Q16 (co-09): Why can profit rise while operating cash falls?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Because profit counts promises while cash counts collections, and working-capital moves wedge between them. Growing receivables and inventory consume cash that sales have already booked as profit. Example 10 bridges 117,000 of profit to 127,000 of cash line by line. Read the cash reconciliation before celebrating any profit figure. Lenders do, which is why loan agreements track operating cash alongside earnings: dividends and repayments clear in cash, never in net income.

</details>

### Q17 (co-10): Why is NPV the deciding rule among project metrics?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

NPV is the only rule that measures absolute wealth created in present-value terms. Positive means owners are richer after repaying cost and hurdle; negative means poorer. Payback ignores value, the index scales it, and IRR restates it as a rate. When rules conflict on mutually exclusive projects, follow the wealth, as Example 26 proves. Positive means the project beats its hurdle after repaying its cost; negative means it destroys wealth even when its cash totals look generous.

</details>

### Q18 (co-10): What two mistakes does year-indexing prevent in NPV code?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Starting the enumeration at zero discounts year one's cash not at all, and forgetting to subtract the cost reports value instead of net value. Example 22 starts at one and subtracts cost explicitly. Off-by-one timing is the classic NPV bug because the loop looks right while every slice is wrong. Verify the first slice by hand on every new schedule. The enumeration in Example 22 starts at one on purpose, and forgetting to subtract the cost reports value instead of net value: two small habits that prevent the two classic NPV bugs.

</details>

### Q19 (co-11): What assumption about reinvestment hides inside IRR?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

IRR assumes interim cash reinvests at the IRR itself, which flatters fast small projects with unrealistic rates. NPV assumes reinvestment at the hurdle, the honest rate. That is why Example 26's small project wins on IRR at 25.69 percent while losing on wealth. Quote IRR with its reinvestment assumption attached, or it will mislead exactly when projects differ most. NPV reinvests at the hurdle instead, which is why the two rules can crown different winners from identical inputs.

</details>

### Q20 (co-12): Why is equity dearer than debt even though it has no coupon?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Equity holders are paid last and bear the most risk, so they demand the highest return: 10.60 percent against 5.25 percent after-tax debt at Merpati. The coupon is a payment schedule, not a cost ranking. Treating retained earnings as free invites overinvestment. Every hurdle must price equity explicitly, or cheap-feeling funding will fund weak projects. Beta is the only firm-specific input: estimate it from comparable listed firms, never from optimism about your own steadiness.

</details>

### Q21 (co-13): Why must WACC weights come from market values?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Because WACC prices the claims investors hold today, and markets reprice those claims continuously while books record history. Book-weighted blends overweight stale values and corrupt the hurdle. Merpati's two-thirds equity weight comes from a 15.20 share price, not the 500,000 book figure. Refresh weights when prices move materially. Book equity of 500,000 against market equity of 760,000 shows how far the two can diverge: the book blend would underweight equity and silently lower the hurdle.

</details>

### Q22 (co-14): What does a coverage covenant protect, and when does it bind?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

It protects lenders by capping total debt as a multiple of operating profit, and it binds in bad years when EBIT falls while debt stays. Merpati's 3.00 floor caps debt at 857,142.86 in a good year but far less in a trough. Borrowing to the ceiling at a peak is how firms breach in the valley. Treat headroom as insurance, not capacity. Dividing profit by the required cover converts any covenant into a hard debt ceiling in two steps, which turns vague borrowing room into a number the board can monitor.

</details>

### Q23 (co-15): Why do DCF and multiple valuations disagree, and which do you trust?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

They disagree because DCF values the firm's own forecasted cash while multiples borrow the market's pricing of peers. Merpati's DCF says 1,471,428.57 while its 6.50 P/E only enables comparison. Trust the DCF for decisions, because its assumptions are inspectable, and use multiples to flag candidates. Never buy on a multiple alone. A low multiple can mean cheap or troubled, and the number alone never says which; use multiples to flag candidates, then value the flagged ones properly with cash flows.

</details>

### Q24 (co-16): What is a margin of safety, and what does it forgive?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Margin of safety is the discount to your own valuation: the share of your number you refuse to pay. Merpati's 11.65 percent at a 1,300,000 asking price forgives wrong forecasts without flipping the decision. It turns valuation from prediction into discipline. Demand it whenever inputs are guesses, which is whenever they are forecasts.

</details>

### Kata 01 solution: annuity present value

**After** (`drilling/code/kata-01-annuity-present-value/after/kata.py`)

```python
# Kata 01 solution: value a five-year 2,000 annuity at 8 percent.
# => Merpati Trading Co. is fictional; every number here is fixed.
# => The factor converts the stream into one present sum.
from decimal import Decimal

# => The equal yearly payment and the discount rate.
payment = Decimal("2000")  # => five identical receipts
rate = Decimal("0.08")  # => 8 percent demanded return
years = 5  # => the stream length
# => One plus the rate, raised to the stream length.
growth = (Decimal("1") + rate) ** years  # => shared compounding base
# => Annuity factor: one minus the inverse growth, over the rate.
factor = (Decimal("1") - Decimal("1") / growth) / rate  # => 3.99 at 8 percent
# => The stream value is the payment times the factor.
value = payment * factor  # => one number for five payments
# => About 7,985.42: the whole stream priced as one sum.
print("value:", value.quantize(Decimal("0.01")))  # => Output is the stream value
```

**Output** (`drilling/code/kata-01-annuity-present-value/expected/main.stdout.txt`):

```text

```

# Kata 02 solution: blend 10.60 percent equity and 5.25 percent debt.
# => Merpati Trading Co. is fictional; every number here is fixed.
# => Market weights, not book weights, carry the blend.
from decimal import Decimal

# => Component costs from the capstone's funding mix.
equity_cost = Decimal("0.106")  # => CAPM cost of equity
debt_cost = Decimal("0.0525")  # => after-tax cost of debt
# => Total market funding is the weighting base.
total = Decimal("760000") + Decimal("380000")  # => every funding unit

equity_weight = Decimal("760000") / total  # => two-thirds equity
debt_weight = Decimal("1") - equity_weight  # => exact complement debt
# => WACC is each cost scaled by its weight, summed.
wacc = equity_weight * equity_cost + debt_weight * debt_cost  # => the blend
# => 8.82 percent: the hurdle the whole firm must clear.

### Kata 03 solution: NPV from a cash-flow schedule

**After** (`drilling/code/kata-03-npv-from-cash-flow-schedule/after/kata.py`)

```python
# Kata 03 solution: NPV a 240,000 van against five yearly inflows.
# => Merpati Trading Co. is fictional; every number here is fixed.
# => Year indexes start at one: year one discounts exactly once.
from decimal import Decimal

# => The outlay, the hurdle, and the five inflows.
cost = Decimal("240000")  # => paid today
rate = Decimal("0.10")  # => 10 percent demanded return
flows = [Decimal("70000"), Decimal("80000"), Decimal("90000")]  # => first three
flows += [Decimal("90000"), Decimal("70000")]  # => last two
# => Discount each inflow by its year and accumulate the total.
discounted = Decimal("0")  # => running sum of present values
for year, cash in enumerate(flows, start=1):  # => year-indexed discounting
    discounted += cash / ((Decimal("1") + rate) ** year)  # => one slice of value
# => Net present value is discounted inflows minus the outlay.
npv = discounted - cost  # => positive accepts the van
# => About 62,300: the van creates value above the hurdle.
print("npv:", npv.quantize(Decimal("0.01")))  # => Output is the verdict number
```

### Kata 05 solution: sensitivity table

**After** (`drilling/code/kata-05-sensitivity-table/after/kata.py`)

```python
# Kata 05 solution: print the van's NPV at 8, 10, and 12 percent.
# => Merpati Trading Co. is fictional; every number here is fixed.
# => The table shows value falling as the demanded return rises.
from decimal import Decimal

# => The outlay and the five inflows.
cost = Decimal("240000")  # => paid today
flows = [Decimal("70000"), Decimal("80000"), Decimal("90000")]  # => first three
flows += [Decimal("90000"), Decimal("70000")]  # => last two
# => One NPV per rung on the sensitivity ladder.
rates = [Decimal("0.08"), Decimal("0.10"), Decimal("0.12")]  # => low to high
for rate in rates:  # => one valuation per demanded return
    # => Discount every inflow at this rung and subtract the cost.
    total = Decimal("0")  # => running present value
    for year, cash in enumerate(flows, start=1):  # => year-indexed discounting
        total += cash / ((Decimal("1") + rate) ** year)  # => one discounted slice
    # => Each rung prints its own verdict number.
    print("rate:", rate, "npv:", (total - cost).quantize(Decimal("0.01")))  # => Output
```

## Elaborative interrogation & self-explanation

### Why 1: Why Decimal for money instead of float, and when would float be acceptable?

Money rounds to cents under law and contract, and binary floats cannot represent most decimals exactly, so repeated float arithmetic drifts by fractions of a cent that compound across schedules. Decimal with explicit quantization keeps every intermediate exact and rounds once at presentation. The rejected alternative, float everywhere with round at the end, hides drift instead of preventing it. Float is acceptable only for throwaway sketches where no output is recorded or compared; every committed number in this course uses Decimal. Self-explain further: quantization is a contract with the reader about precision, and placing it at the print boundary keeps the arithmetic honest while the display stays conventional. If a reviewer asks why a total ends in exactly two decimals, the answer is one line of code, not a paragraph of apology.

### Why 2: Why market weights instead of book weights in WACC, and when would book win?

WACC prices the claims investors hold today, and markets reprice continuously while books freeze history at cost. Book weights would judge live decisions by dead prices, overweighting whichever claim appreciated least. The rejected alternative is simpler data entry: books are printed, markets must be looked up. Book wins only when no market exists, such as a wholly private firm with no observable price, and then the weights should be labeled estimates rather than measurements. Self-explain further: labeling an estimate as an estimate is what lets the sensitivity analysis in Theme I do its job, because a known guess can be stressed while a hidden one cannot. The WACC input audit in Example 33 cannot catch a wrong-but-plausible book weight, so the label is the only defense.

### Why 3: Why NPV over IRR for mutually exclusive projects, and when does IRR decide?

NPV measures absolute wealth while IRR measures rate, and owners spend wealth, not rates. IRR's hidden reinvestment assumption flatters small fast projects, as Example 26 shows. The rejected alternative, always trusting the higher rate, picks the smaller gain. IRR decides only for independent projects under no capital constraint, where every return above the hurdle can run; under choice or rationing, NPV ranks and the index rations. Self-explain further: the two rules share all their inputs and differ only in the question they answer, so running both costs one extra function call and buys the conflict check of Example 26 for free. A project where both agree is decided twice, which is exactly the redundancy a recommendation wants.

### Why 4: Why does payback survive if it ignores the time value of money?

Payback answers the liquidity question NPV never asks: how fast does cash come home. A firm with tight credit can die waiting for a high-NPV project's distant years, so speed is a genuine constraint. The rejected alternative, NPV-only screening, approves brilliant projects the firm cannot survive to collect. Keep payback as a pre-screen with an explicit cutoff, never as the verdict; it kills obvious losers and describes risk, then stands aside. Self-explain further: stating the cutoff in the policy, such as four years for equipment, turns a vague preference for speed into an auditable rule. Example 24's three-year payback passes any reasonable cutoff, which is why the example can move on to value instead of relitigating speed.

### Why 5: Why state assumptions on a receipt instead of embedding them in prose?

Prose buries numbers in sentences where challengers cannot find or change them; a receipt lists every input in one testable place. Example 43's five lines let anyone reprice the firm in minutes when growth or rates move. The rejected alternative, narrative assumptions, turns every dispute into archaeology. Write the receipt first and the argument second, so disagreement becomes arithmetic instead of rhetoric. Self-explain further: the receipt also versions the decision, because reprinting it next quarter shows exactly which input moved the verdict. A valuation without a dated receipt cannot be revisited, only replaced; with one, updating is a diff instead of a debate.

### Why 6: Why three gates instead of one verdict in a recommendation?

One verdict hides which part of the argument carries the decision, so a broken input can ride a strong one to approval. Example 48's gates separate value, return, and survival, and each can fail independently: NPV can clear while coverage collapses. The rejected alternative, a single approve line, is faster to write and impossible to audit. Gate the decision so reviewers can disagree with one gate without rejecting the analysis. Self-explain further: gates compose across decisions, because the same value-return-survival triple frames the warehouse, the van, and the capital structure. Learning one pattern that transfers to every later choice is the whole return on the course; the examples were just the practice ground.
